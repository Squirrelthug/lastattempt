!!! know "What Blizzard confirmed"
    No flying, no level scaling, no boosts, no WoW Token — "the catch-up system is simple: play the game." The cooking fire becomes a campsite with vendors, repairs, profession workspaces and one-hour buffs you have to sit down for. Over 600 new recipes, crafted food that grants XP, and 1000+ new quests across the existing 1–60 zones. Gear can carry conditional effects — more damage to a creature type, more defense in a biome.

As a design philosophy, Blizzard has never really moved away from the world as a character. Initially it was the main character, and the players were simply *a* hero, not *the* hero. Somewhere in World of Warcraft's history — when we transitioned from another hero fighting for the cause to the hero referenced in cinematics and requested by Thrall himself — we pulled away from our focus on the world.

That's not a failure of craft. Blizzard has always held a high standard for world-building: the foliage placed everywhere, the roads, the NPCs, the mobs, the rare spawns. All of it contributes to a lush world that feels lived in. Some expansions have done this better than others. Mists of Pandaria stood out above so many others, and still does, as an environment that invokes a populated world you're visiting. Blizzard has always had this skill.

## Where the walking went

What changed is the direction the game is built from. Players are no longer walking from one point to the next, and reintroducing that friction in an era where Blizzard spends so much effort reducing friction everywhere else feels like a step backwards every time it comes up. So the approach in most recent content has been to make flying accessible immediately. There are exceptions, but for the most part it's something we can expect as players.

The world is beautiful from up on a mount, flying across zones at speed. It is still very beautiful from the ground. If you ever take the time to stay on a ground mount, or simply walk between objectives while levelling, you will experience a world that is well put together from any angle. But having moved away from walking, and having moved away from being a side character in a cast of characters, the world has lost its place as the main character.

Blizzard talks about journey before destination. Bringing players back to a version of the game where the world is the main character means the journey gets appreciated again, and the exploration and discovery along the way becomes part of it.

It's true that it's inconvenient to walk across a continent, or to idle on a flight path from the north end of Kalimdor to the south. It also gives you time to talk to your guild, organize your bags, tab out and check Discord. Players find plenty of ways to kill time when the distance is real. It was inconvenient, it did cause friction — and it was part of the experience. It's something we've lost in modern WoW.

## Never, on purpose

Promising to never bring back flying mounts, never sell boosts, never sell a WoW Token — that reads as a very intentional choice to isolate Forever from the other versions and keep the journey more important than the destination. Not being able to shortcut anything is what protects what this world represents: horizontal progression.

There are examples players point to, Guild Wars being the usual one, of horizontal progression systems that players genuinely enjoy. The developers on these panels were describing very conditional effects on armor and weapons — extra damage or extra defense against a species or an enemy type, or in an environment, so you hit harder in a desert or hold up better in a cave. There's a lot of room to build gear into interesting choices that keep horizontal progression persisting for as long as the game does.

## The campfire as an open invitation

Campfires — a replacement for cooking, or really an extension of it — are supposed to make players more social. It doesn't force anyone to communicate. But it creates a situation where you can *find* people. You can see very clearly when there are fewer than five players sitting at a fire. That might be their whole party. It would be a really good idea, for any player, to ask if there's room to tag along and quest with them.

Normally it's hard to find a group in one location, not moving. People are spread across the zone on their own objectives, going back to town to clear bags, meeting up at the dungeon while trying to fill roles. Being able to set out a campfire that reads as an open invitation changes that. For the players willing to reach out, it's going to be much easier to fall into a cooperative style of play than to play alone.

World of Warcraft can be played alone, and there's something to be said for that — it isn't necessary to be in a group to enjoy levelling. But to enjoy the majority of the game, a group is more or less required. I can see this leading to a lot of friendships, a lot of meetings, and the serendipitous encounters the developers said they were hoping for.

Add the 600 new recipes across every profession, crafted food that grants an XP bonus, and new quests in *all* the zones rather than just the new ones, and every piece of it goes toward the same thing: supporting the world as the main character. The more horizontal development we get, the more that pays off.

!!! open "What nobody knows yet"
    Whether people actually sit down. The campfire only works as a social system if enough players treat it as an invitation rather than a buff dispenser — and that's a culture question the beta can't answer.

That's the bet. Friction was never the point; it was the cost of a world you had to move through. Forever is paying that cost on purpose, and the campfire is the first place we'll find out whether players still think it's worth it.
